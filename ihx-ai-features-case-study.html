The actual capability, not the four features

What I focus on in this case study is not the four features themselves. It is the underlying design capability: knowing where inside an existing, legacy SaaS product AI genuinely helps, what historical data already sitting in the product can be reused instead of built from scratch, and how to design the human-AI handoff so the person on the other end actually trusts the output enough to rely on it. Each section below applies that capability to one real point of failure.

02 · BenefSnap.AI · Beneficiary Search

Manual beneficiary search was 37% slower to fail than to succeed.

BenefSnap digitizes member card information and performs automated payer search validation before submission, replacing manual parameter entry with document upload and extraction.

+37%
Slower to resolve a failed beneficiary search (2h21m) than a successful one (1h40m median)
1 Failed searches route to a manual queue

Someone maps the correct beneficiary ID by hand, or the hospital falls back to offline email submission.

2 Hospitals were pushing back entirely

On doing beneficiary search manually at all: friction severe enough to threaten adoption of the step itself.

The decision

There were two ways to solve this: have the patient enter their own policy details, or give the TPA desk a way to stop typing altogether. I designed for the second, because the underlying data already existed on a document the desk already handles: the member card, policy card, or e-card the patient hands over at the counter. Rather than asking the desk to read that card and retype it, the design moves document upload earlier in the flow and lets extraction do the retyping.

Building trust
01
It degrades gracefully

If the hospital has the complete docket at intimation, the system runs a full beneficiary search immediately. If they only have a member or policy card, that's enough to proceed, saved in draft until the rest arrives. Never an all-or-nothing document requirement.

02
It sits inside the existing step, not a new one

Document upload was placed at the point where TPA users already select a payer pair, not bolted on as an additional screen, so adopting the AI-assisted path did not mean learning a new process.

03
The output is auditable

Extracted member name, policy number, insurer, and TPA auto-fill the form, but nothing is submitted invisibly. The desk sees exactly what was extracted before it goes to the payer.

03 · CodeRight.AI · Diagnosis & ICD Coding

61% of diagnosis entries carried no ICD-10 code, and the person typing them isn't a clinician.

CodeRight lets hospital staff enter a diagnosis in natural language and maps that language to a ranked set of ICD-10 code suggestions, instead of requiring the user to search a clinical coding system directly.

61%
Of 1.8–2 lakh monthly diagnosis entries carry no ICD-10 code
61% uncoded39% coded
1 The user isn't a clinician

TPA desk staff know a patient's condition in plain words, like "stone in kidney," not ICD terminology. Doctors themselves use shorthand ("TM perf") a literal search would miss entirely.

2 A dropdown assumes what's missing

A searchable ICD dropdown assumes the user already knows the clinical term they're searching for, exactly what this user does not have.

49% usable free text, nothing selected 12% garbage input 21% wrong code picked outright
The decision

I did not build a searchable ICD dropdown. Instead, the diagnosis field stays free text, in the user's own words, and AI maps that language to ICD candidates behind the scenes using lingo and synonym matching, hospital-specific usage patterns, and semantic search. The interface never asks the user to think like a medical coder.

Building trust
01
Multi-select, not auto-fill

The system never picks a code for the user. It surfaces up to five ranked ICD suggestions and requires a deliberate selection, because a wrong auto-selected code on a claims document is worse than a slower but correct one.

02
Case-volume as a trust signal

Each suggestion shows how many times that specific hospital has submitted that diagnosis before, alongside a confidence label (high, medium, low), evidence to weigh against their own institutional memory.

03
Naming the failure mode, not hiding it

The interface states plainly that AI suggestions may not reflect every insurer's specific requirements and should be verified before submitting, at the exact moment the user is deciding whether to rely on it.

04 · DocuSense.AI · Document Intelligence

Hospitals learned what a payer wanted only when the query arrived, often after discharge.

DocuSense generates a dynamic, claim-specific document checklist at upload time, based on the diagnosis and procedure already entered, and shows the hospital a live estimate of how likely this claim is to trigger a payer query.

The problem

Hospitals had no way to know what documents a payer would actually ask for until the query arrived days later, often after the patient had already been discharged and the paperwork was harder to retrieve. The checklist requirement was effectively invisible until it was too late to act on cheaply.

The decision

Rather than a static, one-size-fits-all upload checklist, the required-documents list is generated dynamically from the ICD code and procedure already captured for that specific claim, using the same mapping logic underneath CodeRight. The checklist exists without asking the hospital for any new input; it reuses data the claim already has.

Building trust
01
Risk made visible before submission, not after

A query-probability indicator turns an abstract downstream risk into a concrete, present-moment number the user can act on immediately, instead of finding out days later.

"There is a probability of receiving a query on this claim," shown above the checklist (35% here is an illustrative example, not a reported figure)
02
Documents grouped by why they matter

Grouped stage-specific vs. ICD-specific, so the user understands the reasoning behind each requirement instead of facing an undifferentiated list.

03
The same disclaimer, on purpose

The exact "based on historical patterns, please verify" language used in CodeRight appears here too, one consistent mental model for what an AI suggestion means on IHX, not a different trust convention per feature.

05 · QueryLens.AI · Query Categorization & Routing

Categorization was implicit, routing was tribal knowledge, resolution was fragmented across four tools.

QueryLens reads an incoming payer query, classifies what kind of query it is, suggests who on the hospital's team should own it, and gives the hospital an in-platform chat and structured reply flow, so the whole resolution happens inside IHX instead of across WhatsApp, email, and a payer portal.

CategorizeImplicit

Desk reads free text and decides mentally, with no system record of that decision

→
RouteTribal knowledge

"Call Priya for billing," a mapping that breaks the moment shifts change

→
ResolveFragmented

Word doc, HIS, payer portal, WhatsApp: none of which talk to each other

Result: queries sat unresolved for 12 to 48 hours, not because the answer was hard, but because the coordination was, and an audit trail was nearly impossible to reconstruct after the fact.

The decision

Rather than treating categorization, routing, and resolution as three separate problems, I designed them as one continuous workflow: AI categorizes the query, the system suggests the right team members before the desk has to think about it, the conversation happens inside the platform, and the reply, with documents, is submitted from the same screen. The desk never has to leave the workflow to resolve a query.

Building trust: a different kind, about visible learning over time
01
Primary and secondary category, shown together, everywhere

Early versions showed only the primary category. Field review found "Medical Necessity" alone too ambiguous to act on; "Medical Necessity: Surgery / Procedure Justification" told hospitals exactly what the payer wanted, a direct hospital-feedback-driven change.

02
The system shows its own learning, live

When a query the AI could not categorize gets manually assigned by the desk, the category visibly upgrades on the row and in the timeline, from a grey "Others" pill to the chosen department's icon and color. Hidden learning is not trustworthy learning.

03
Two distinct correction signals, not one

A correction on an already-categorized claim and a first categorization of an "Others" claim answer different questions, so they're logged separately: one training precision, one training recall, rather than averaged into one vague "override."
